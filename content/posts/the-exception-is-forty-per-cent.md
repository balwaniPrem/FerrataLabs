There is a moment in every scoping conversation where somebody describes the process, and then adds that there are a few exceptions, but they are rare.

They are almost never rare.

## Where the number comes from

Ask for the last three months of transactions and sort them by whether they went through the standard path. The clean path is usually smaller than anyone in the room believes. A three-way match that everyone describes as automatic turns out to have partial receipts, price tolerances, freight allocated after the fact, and a supplier whose invoices arrive as one line for a whole month.

None of those are exceptions in any meaningful sense. They are the work.

The reason the estimate is always wrong is that the people describing the process are describing the version they designed, not the version that runs. Nobody is being dishonest. The exceptions are handled by three people who have absorbed them into their working day, and absorbed work is invisible work.

## Why it decides the outcome

An agent built for the clean path will handle the clean path. If the clean path is sixty percent of volume, the team keeps every person they had, adds a system to supervise, and correctly concludes that the pilot did not pay for itself.

An agent built for the actual distribution looks different. It handles more cases, it refuses more clearly when it should not proceed, and its queue is full of the things that used to be somebody's afternoon.

This is the entire reason the engagement opens with two weeks of measurement rather than a workshop. Not because measurement is virtuous, but because the shape of the distribution determines whether there is a project at all.

## What to do with the answer

Sometimes the honest read is that the workflow is not a good candidate. The exceptions are genuinely varied, each one needs judgment, and what the team actually needs is a cleaner supplier master rather than an agent.

Saying so costs a project and buys the only thing worth having in the second meeting, which is that the numbers came from somewhere.
